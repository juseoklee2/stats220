YouTube Vlog Strategy: What the Data Reveals

Juseok Lee

Introduction

NEW Title Language   NEW Comment Counts

This visual data story extends my YouTube observation project from Project 2, now expanded with two new variables: Title Language (Korean vs. non-Korean) and Comment Counts. These additions allow a more complete picture of audience engagement — views alone tell us about reach, but comment counts reveal active participation. Title language lets me explore whether linguistic choices shape who watches and how they interact.

The data was collected by personally observing and logging YouTube videos over time through a Google Form. Timestamps were recorded automatically, enabling day-of-week analysis using the {lubridate} package. Title language was classified programmatically using str_detect() from {stringr}, ensuring consistency across all entries.

The story unfolds in three chapters: which video type draws the most viewers, whether the language of a title affects comment engagement relative to views, and which day of the week is associated with the highest average reach.

1. Which Video Type Attracts the Most Views?

The first question any creator asks is: what kind of content actually performs? To answer this, I grouped all logged videos by their category and calculated the average view count per type.

Horizontal bar chart showing average views by video category. Bars are ordered from highest to lowest average views, coloured with the Set2 palette. The chart highlights which video types achieve the greatest audience reach on average.

The chart reveals a clear ranking of content performance. Certain video categories consistently outperform others in average views, suggesting that topic choice — not just upload frequency — plays a decisive role in audience reach. This finding directly informs which content types are worth prioritising.

2. Do Korean Titles Drive More Comments?

For this second analysis, I combined both new variables: comment counts and title language. Each point represents one logged video, with the trend line showing the relationship between views and comments — split by whether the title was in Korean or not.

Title language was identified automatically using str_detect(), making the classification reproducible regardless of how many rows are added to the dataset in future.

Scatter plot with a linear trend line showing the relationship between total views (x-axis) and number of comments (y-axis). Points are coloured by title language: Korean Title vs Non-Korean Title. Two trend lines reveal whether comment engagement scales differently with views depending on the language used.

The slope of each trend line tells the real story: if Korean-titled videos have a steeper rise in comments per view, it suggests a more engaged — perhaps more loyal — audience. A shallower slope for non-Korean titles may indicate broader but more passive reach. This distinction matters when deciding whether to optimise for depth of engagement or width of exposure.

3. When Should You Upload? A Day-of-Week Analysis

The final visualisation uses the automatically recorded Timestamp variable. By extracting the day of the week with wday() from {lubridate}, I can identify whether certain days are associated with higher average views across all logged videos.

Bar chart showing average views for each day of the week, from Monday to Sunday. Bars are coloured using the Set2 palette. The chart is used to identify which days tend to produce the highest average audience reach based on the observation timestamps.

Not all days are equal. The chart highlights specific days where average views are notably higher, pointing to windows where uploading — or at minimum, observing — is most likely to catch audience attention. A data-driven upload schedule built on findings like these can meaningfully improve a channel’s growth trajectory.
